# Who used ToolBook, and who would care now

Research notes, 2026-10-13. Names below are organizations in published sources. They are not people from this project's mail. Graded claims are WV-029 and WV-030.

## What the product was

Asymetrix shipped ToolBook in 1990. Paul Allen founded the company after leaving Microsoft. The first versions were a Windows authoring tool in the same breath as HyperCard and, for a while, Visual Basic. The book metaphor is the product: a project is a book, a screen is a page, and OpenScript is the language.

The training edition arrived with ToolBook 3 in 1994. Question objects, scoring, and later an LMS (Librarian, then Ingenium) sat on top of the same book. Click2learn renamed the company. SumTotal Systems was the merger with Docent in 2004. The last ToolBook was 11.5, in December 2012. SumTotal ended sales and support on 31 December 2021.

Two products shared the name. Assistant was templates and no programming. Instructor was OpenScript. Native content was the Windows runtime these lessons use. DHTML content was the later web export. A remark about "ToolBook users" that does not say which of those it means is not about `TB80RTM`.

## Who published sources actually name

These are contemporary accounts, not a census. A press release is a press release.

**Known from the books in this project.** Nida Corporation wrote the electronics lessons for Navy advanced technical training and shipped them as ToolBook II 8.1 shells. That use is not a clipping. The copyright pages and the lesson text are in the files.

**Named in trade press as users or pilots of the authoring tools.**

- Northrop Grumman, under an Air Force aircrew-training contract, adopted ToolBook II Assistant 6.5 to move faster-changing lessons off an older simulation system. An instructional designer there described video synchronized to highlighted text, aimed at manufacturing training. (FCW, November 1998.)
- The Navy. In 1997 Asymetrix demonstrated a ToolBook II lesson taken by a sailor on the USS Princeton, with results sent by satellite to Dahlgren, Virginia, and the company. The integrator named in the piece was Advanced Acoustics Concepts. The same season, Asymetrix had bought AimTech and allied with Raytheon E-Systems on Navy course modernization. (FCW, November 1997.)
- Naval Professional Development and Technology Center, Pensacola. ToolBook II was one of four authoring tools in a project for designers of Navy technical courses. (FCW, December 1996.)
- Air Education and Training Command, Randolph Air Force Base, planning a pilot of Asymetrix course management and a set of military-police modules. That article is about Librarian as much as about the authoring tool. (FCW, April 1998.)
- Buckman Laboratories, a specialty-chemical company, used ToolBook II Instructor and Ingenium for a learning center. The company's own case study claims 9,000 hours of multilingual training to 1,242 people in 1998. Treat the hour counts as the company's number.
- A 1994 UK ToolBook User Conference, and a Bristol medical-school paper on building lessons from templates without scripting. There was a user community outside the US military.

**Marketing, not a customer list.** A 1999 Asymetrix page said companies like AT&T, Lucent, Metropolitan Life, and Boeing were doing online learning with the company's tools. "Like" is doing a lot of work. That sentence is not evidence those firms ran ToolBook II 8.1, or any particular version.

The Department of Defense was, in Asymetrix's own telling, one of the largest markets for this kind of training software. The interesting part for this project is narrower. The lessons on disk are one contractor's electronics curriculum for the Navy, built on the native 8.1 runtime, not the DHTML export and not Librarian.

## Who would care now

Not a new generation of authors. The product is dead. Assistant was already retired before the end. Nobody needs a pitch for writing next year's course in OpenScript.

The people who still have a reason:

- Anyone holding an 8.x book they cannot open. That is a 16-bit runtime on a 64-bit machine, which does not start. A 32-bit Windows XP virtual machine still runs it. A reader that does not need the virtual machine is the other way through the same door. The Nida electronics set is the case this project is for. The same door fits other native 8.x CBT that was distributed on CD and then stranded.
- A school or a contractor that bought the Nida trainer and still has the discs. The trainer half is the part that talked to Nida's own systems. The lesson half is the books.
- An author who still has the `.tbk` and wants the text, the figures, and the questions out. ToolBook 11.5's `.TBKX` export is the vendor's own answer to that, if the old book opens there. It has not been tried on these lessons. See WV-009.
- Preservation. ToolBook is a 1990 Windows authoring line with a real user conference, a named language, and a body of training material that will not run on a current desktop. That is a software-history problem, not a product problem.

Indifference is the honest default for everyone else. A modern course writer does not gain anything by learning OpenScript. A SumTotal LMS customer in 2026 is not a ToolBook 8.1 user. The ledger should not grow a claim that "the industry wants ToolBook back." It does not.
